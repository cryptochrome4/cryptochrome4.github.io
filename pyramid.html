【Part 1】Beyond the Alphabet: The Three Distinct Roles of Hieroglyphs

Introduction

Through your study, you have built an excellent foundation matching individual letters to specific sounds.

However, when facing genuine tomb murals, words often don't seem to connect logically. This occurs because ancient Egyptians assigned three separate roles to their symbols. In this section, we will master these three fundamental roles to decode real inscriptions.

The Three Roles of Hieroglyphs

Hieroglyphic signs on tomb walls do not always function like our standard alphabet. Depending on the context, a single sign will play one of these three roles:

1. Phonograms

These signs ignore the actual object drawn and represent only a specific sound. Your current alphabet chart works this way.

2. Ideograms

These signs directly represent the literal meaning of the object. A sun sketch means "sun," and a foot means "foot."

3. Determinatives

Placed at the absolute end of a word, these classify the general category or meaning group. These signs are completely silent.

Why Do We Need Determinatives?

Ancient Egyptian writing omits vowels (A, I, U, E, O) entirely; only consonants are recorded.

For example, if you wrote just the consonants "K-T" in English, it could mean "cat," "kite," "coat," or "cut." To solve this ambiguity, Egyptians added an unpronounced visual clue (a determinative) at the end of the word.

💡 Practical Examples of Determinatives

  • Walking Legs : Added to words related to movement, traveling, running, or walking.
  • Person with Hand to Mouth : Added to words related to eating, drinking, speaking, or thinking.
  • Papyrus Scroll : Added to words representing knowledge, abstract thoughts, or laws.

Scribes utilized these categories to make sure anyone could read the wall murals without misinterpreting the spoken language.

【Part 2】The Masters of the Mural: "Biliteral & Triliteral Signs" and the Square Rule

The Most Common Signs on the Tomb Walls

As mentioned in Part 1, Egyptian wall murals are rarely written using only single alphabet letters. To save valuable space and create balanced designs, the ancient scribes relied heavily on signs that represent multiple consonant sounds at once.

Below are the most crucial biliteral and triliteral signs that appear repeatedly in royal tombs like Queen Nefertari's. Mastering these will immediately help you decode real artifacts!

① Essential Biliteral Signs (1 Character = 2 Sounds)

Glyph Image Motif Sounds Common Meaning
House floor plan pr (per) House, palace, to go out
Game board mn (men) To establish, enduring, stable
Seal ring / Rope loop šn (shen) To encircle, eternity (The origin of the cartouche)
Owl m (em) In, by means of, from (Active as both 1 and 2 consonant signs)

② Sacred Triliteral Signs (1 Character = 3 Sounds)

Glyph Image Motif Sounds Common Meaning
Tied strap / Mirror ꜥnḫ (ankh) Life, to live (The most crucial word!)
Offering table ḥtp (hotep) Peace, contentment, offering
Dung beetle ḫpr (kheper) To become, to exist, manifestation
Eye of Horus wdj (udja) Sound, whole, protected (Amulet against evil)

The Aesthetic of Alignment: "The Square Rule"

When arranging hieroglyphs, ancient Egyptian scribes followed a strict artistic guideline: all characters must be beautifully packed into invisible square blocks.

Scribes absolutely detested empty spaces. Instead of letting characters stretch out in a long, uneven row, they stacked and grouped signs horizontally and vertically to fill the layout completely.

💡 The Visual Packing Trick

For instance, after writing the game board sign mn, they would often place a small water ripple sign n directly underneath it to cleanly fill the rectangular gap.

This extra "n" does not mean you pronounce a double sound; it is simply a visual filler to confirm the pronunciation and achieve a perfect balance. When inspecting murals, always look for how characters are perfectly boxed together!

【Part 3】First Steps in Grammar: Sentences Starting with Actions

The Mystery of Word Order

Even if you know how to read individual signs, you cannot decipher a sentence without understanding its structure. The word order in Ancient Egyptian is completely different from modern languages.

While English uses "Subject + Verb + Object" (I visit Egypt), an Ancient Egyptian sentence almost always starts with the Action (Verb).

The Hieroglyphic Sentence Structure: VSO Type

1. Verb (V)
The action comes first (e.g., "Gives" or "Presents").
2. Subject (S)
Who performs the action comes second (e.g., "the King" or "the Goddess").
3. Object (O)
What receives the action comes last (e.g., "eternal life" or "offerings").

💡 How It Looks on a Tomb Mural

A very common phrase like "The King gives life to the God Osiris" is written following the exact VSO order. If you translate the signs directly in sequence, it reads like this:

"Gives (Verb) ＋ the King (Subject) ＋ life (Object) ＋ to Osiris (Bonus Info)"

Training your eyes to spot the action word first will instantly guide you on where to begin reading a wall column!

Word Genders: Masculine vs. Feminine

Every single noun (objects, titles, and names) in Ancient Egyptian has a grammatical gender, split into masculine and feminine. This acts as a major clue on murals, telling you whether a text is talking about a Pharaoh (male) or a Queen (female).

Masculine Nouns (The Base Form)

These require no special markers or extra letters. They are written in their standard forms.

Examples: King (nesw), God (netjer), Son (sa).

Feminine Nouns (Marked by a Bread Loaf )

Feminine words always attach the single consonant alphabet sign "t" (represented by a semi-circular bread loaf) to the very end of the word. If you spot this sign, the text is talking about a female subject.

Examples: Queen (neswt), Goddess (netjert), Daughter (sat).

Spot the Female Grammar on Nefertari's Mural!

In Part 2, we looked at the word for "Lord" or "Master," which is written as a basket sign and pronounced "neb" (masculine).

However, when referring to Queen Nefertari or Goddess Hathor, the scribes attached the bread loaf sign t to the end, changing it to "nebet" (Lady / Mistress).

Whenever you see that little bread loaf sign resting near a name, you instantly know: "Ah, this column is honoring a Queen or a Goddess!"

【Part 4】Hunting the Royal and Divine: Spotting Cartouches and Great Gods

The First Thing to Locate: The Cartouche

When you take a look at an Egyptian wall inscription, there is one visual element that you can recognize instantly within one second. It is the "Cartouche" (Royal Name Ring).

In ancient Egypt, the names of Kings (Pharaohs) and great Queens were wrapped inside a protective oval rope loop to shield them from evil spirits and curses. If you spot a long vertical or horizontal oval outline, the text inside is guaranteed to be a royal name, like "Seti" or "Nefertari."

𓋹 The Advantage of Finding a Cartouche 𓋹

Locating a cartouche acts as your perfect landmark! Once you find the oval, you know exactly whose tomb or history you are reading. It is the golden rule of starting any mural decipherment.

How to Recognize Major Gods by Their Symbol Sets

Along with royalty, gods fill up most of the space on tomb walls. Instead of spelling out their names letter-by-letter with your standard alphabet chart, it is much faster to learn their specific signature combinations (fixed sets of signs). Let's master the 4 major gods found on Nefertari's murals!

Osiris (Lord of the Underworld)

Written using the combination of a "Throne" and an "Eye" to spell "Osiris" (Wsjr). On tomb walls, he is typically depicted as a seated, mummified figure with green or black skin.

Anubis (God of Mummification)

Written using the letters for "j-n-p" accompanied by a "Jackal" determinative sign. On walls, he appears near the scales of judgment as a jackal-headed deity.

Hathor (Goddess of Love and Beauty)

Represented by a single grand symbol: a "Falcon enclosed inside a Square House". This directly translates to "House of Horus" (Hwt-Hr). She is the goddess wearing a sun disk crown who welcomes Queen Nefertari.

Thoth (God of Wisdom)

On wall paintings, Thoth has the head of an Ibis bird and is shown writing down records. His name is instantly identified because it always starts with an "Ibis bird resting on a sacred perch" sign.

💡 The Scribe's Honor Rule: "God First"

Ancient Egyptians respected their deities deeply. Whenever a god's name or the word "God" appeared in a sentence, the scribes would deliberately move those symbols to the very front (or top) of the column out of pure respect, even if grammar dictated they should be at the end.

This is called "Honorific Transposition." If you notice a god's name positioned slightly out of regular sentence order, you are seeing this sacred respect rule in action!

【Part 5】The Cheat Codes of Inscriptions: Three Essential Formulaic Patterns

Wall Murals Are Made of Templates

When you stand before massive columns of hieroglyphs, you do not need to feel overwhelmed thinking you have to translate every single sign from scratch.

In reality, roughly 80% of divine dialogue and captions on tomb walls rely on highly repetitive templates, much like standard modern email greetings. Recognizing these common formulas will unlock the meaning of almost any mural instantly!

Let's install the 3 biggest formulaic cheat codes into your decipherment toolkit!

Formula 1 Given Life (di ꜥnḫ)

This ultimate blessing is found 100% of the time directly following a pharaoh's cartouche or at the conclusion of a god's statement.

"Given Life" / "May he/she be given life"

Explanation: This pairs the triangular "To Give" sign (di) with the triliteral "Ankh" sign (life) that we memorized in Part 2. It serves as an eternal wish for the pharaoh to receive endless vital force from the gods.

Formula 2 Lord of the Two Lands (nb tꜥwj)

A grand royal title that is almost always positioned immediately *before* a king's or queen's name loop.

"Lord of the Two Lands"

Explanation: "The Two Lands" refers to Upper and Lower Egypt combined. Scribes represented land by drawing a flat straight line with small pellets below it. By stacking two land signs together, it represents duality. This lets you know a pharaoh's name is coming right up.

Formula 3 True of Voice / Justified (mꜥꜥ-ḫrw)

An honorary post-nominal title appended to the names of deceased individuals who successfully cleared the heavy judgment of the afterlife.

"True of Voice" / "The Justified"

Explanation: Pronounced "ma'a-kheru," this means that the individual's heart balanced perfectly against the feather of truth on Anubis's scales. It acts as a spiritual passport, proving that the person was righteous during their life on earth.

Snapping the Blocks Together

With these formulaic blocks ready, look at how easily we can snap a long wall column into distinct, readable pieces:

[Lord of the Two Lands] ＋ [(Cartouche) Nefertari] ＋ [True of Voice]

When combined into a fluent sentence, it reads:
"The Lord of the Two Lands, Queen Nefertari, the Justified."
